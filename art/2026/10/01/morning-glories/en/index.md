# Room for the bloom

Daily Art · 2026-10-01

## Morning Glories

Suzuki Kiitsu · Early 19th century · A pair of six-panel screens

> Blue flowers follow the openings in gold. Stillness gives abundance room to breathe.

![Green vines and deep blue morning glories curl across gold, their pale throats punctuating broad openings between the leaves.](../source.jpg)

![Green vines and deep blue morning glories curl across gold, their pale throats punctuating broad openings between the leaves.](../source-companion.jpg)

The two screens of one paired work, shown in sequence. Both images are complete; this is not a reconstruction of their installation.

Collection facts | Suzuki Kiitsu (1796–1858); ink, color, and gold leaf on paper. Each image: 178.3 × 379.7 cm. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 54.69.1, .2. Dating follows the collection record.[1]

## Composition | Follow the openings

Before counting flowers, trace the gold. The vines enclose irregular openings, giving empty space a contour of its own: a pool within the foliage, a breath between phrases. Dense clusters enter from the edges; thin stems carry the movement onward. Vertical panel divisions establish a beat that the curling growth repeatedly slips past. No single central bloom commands the whole field. The eye is free to wander.

## Color | Cool clusters, warm ground

Deep blue and green form a neighboring cool color group against a yellow-orange gold ground. Blue and yellow-orange suggest a near-complementary relationship, but reflective gold is not a fixed flat yellow. Value matters just as much: dark blossoms anchor the surface while tiny pale throats make brief, bright stops. Because the highlights stay small, repetition feels measured rather than noisy.

## Light | A surface that answers the room

The leaves cast no consistent long shadows, and the gold describes neither sky nor soil. Depth appears locally, where petals turn from dark blue toward a pale throat. The museum interview explains how gold screens reflect light into an interior.[2] The physical surface therefore responds to position and illumination. A digital photograph shows texture, but cannot reproduce that shifting metallic presence.

## Technique | Pattern with a little volume

The documented materials are ink, color, and gold leaf on paper.[1] In the reproduction, crisp leaf silhouettes gather into broad masses, separated by fine veins. Tonal changes around the flower throats give those flatter masses a counterpoint. From afar, pattern; close up, a suggestion of softness and turning petals. Specific pigments or specialist paint procedures cannot be established from this photograph alone.

## Artist | Decoration meets observation

Kiitsu worked in Edo within the Rinpa tradition. The museum interview identifies Sakai Hoitsu as his teacher and discusses the tension between decorative order and naturalistic description.[2] That context helps explain two simultaneous experiences here: a rhythmic surface seen from across a room, and individual flowers turning in slightly different directions when approached. Neither viewing distance exhausts the work.

## Why it moves | Abundance without pressure

To my eye, the work gives a passing moment of bloom unusual composure. Flowers multiply, yet the intervals remain open. Fine tendrils seem ready to extend beyond the image while the gold holds everything briefly in place. For the beginning of October, this is an editorial invitation to linger at the edge of summer and autumn, not a claim about a current exhibition or a universal flowering season.

## Try it | Design the gaps first

For a poster or photo layout, reserve two connected openings of unequal size before placing anything else. Arrange elements around those edges instead of scattering them evenly. Try warm gold #D8B568 at 60%, leaf green #246348 at 25%, and deep blue #102D59 at 15%, with a tiny extra paper-white highlight. Check hierarchy in grayscale first. These are editorial practice colors and proportions, not measured pigments or areas of the original.
