A wall takes a step

The joints divide the body. The stride holds together.

First the stride. Then the wall.

Start at a distance. The forelegs, belly and hooked tail gather a field of blue-green into one readable animal. Move closer and the joints, glaze losses and mottling begin to surface. That double encounter is what holds me: force from afar, time at close range.

Composition | Movement against a grid

Facing left, the lion nearly spans the rectangle. Its open mouth and extended forepaw make the direction unmistakable; the curled tail gathers the remaining momentum on the right. Blue gaps beneath the belly separate the legs. Horizontal joints act like a steady musical staff, while the diagonals of the limbs change the beat. The body is segmented, yet its contour persists. Our eyes bridge the interruptions and finish the walk.

Colour | Warmth inside a cool field

Neighbouring blues and greens establish a cool analogous field. Ochre in the mane and lower border supplies a warmer register. These blue-greens and yellow ochres are not an exact complementary pair: temperature and value do more of the work. The pale body registers first; dark marks articulate mouth, claws and mane. Mottling lowers the overall saturation, giving the gold weight and the blue-green a mineral quiet. This reading concerns the surviving digital image, not a reconstruction of the original colours.

Light | A contour with thickness

Thin highlights trace raised areas of the face and legs; recesses and joints deepen into shadow. Relief and photographic lighting produce these effects. Glossy glints alternate with matte losses, making the eye pause between smoothness and abrasion. The photograph alone cannot establish the lighting setup or identify the extent of every restoration.

Making and makers | A shared scale

The museum dates this Babylonian panel to ca. 604–562 BCE and records ceramic and glaze, accession 31.13.1. Molded glazed bricks brought relief and colour into architecture. Individual makers are unrecorded. The panel belongs to the context of lion decoration along Babylon’s Processional Way: the lion was associated with Ishtar and carried protective and ritual meaning. Its authorship is best approached through craft and architectural collaboration; a personal biography would be invention. These historical details follow the museum record.

Why it moves me | Damage without collapse

For me, the moving quality is the way a complete gesture accommodates an injured surface. The bricks expose their joints; the animal keeps its force. We see a creature advancing and an object that has survived a long span of time, both at once. As a visual lesson today, it offers restraint: give the clearest contour the expressive task, and let the material speak in its own texture.

Try it | A blue-green palette with a job

For a poster or outfit, try 60% deep teal #17636A as the field, 25% warm ivory #DDD7BD as the main shape, 10% ochre #AD873A for emphasis and 5% ink #243C3C for edges. These are editorial practice colours, not sampled measurements or ancient recipes. Think teal coat, ivory layer and a small ochre accessory. In a layout, carry one continuous silhouette across a modular grid. Check it at phone-thumbnail size before adding texture. Each module needs to support the larger gesture.
